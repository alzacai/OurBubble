# The shadow

> **Scope.** This is a book about a **toy**: a small world built inside a computer, and what happened
> when we pointed real tests at it. Nothing here is a claim about nature. Where a chapter uses a word
> like *vacuum*, the word names a pattern in the model.

<!-- beat the-shadow.1 -->

A child stands in a yard at noon and her shadow is a stub around her feet. She comes back after
dinner and it is longer than she is, stretched across the grass toward the fence.

Nothing about that is strange. Everyone has seen it. What is worth noticing is that she has just
found something that *changes* — reliably, in the same direction, every day of her life — and that
she is under no obligation to do anything about it at all. Most of us do not.

But suppose she does. What is the smallest next thing she could possibly do?

## Two marks in the ground

<!-- beat the-shadow.2 -->

She pushes a stick into the ground and scratches a line in the dirt where its shadow ends. Then she
is called in for lunch, and when she comes back out the shadow has swung across the ground, so she
scratches a second line where it ends now.

The wait is one she already knows by heart. Lunch is a clock a child already owns: it comes once, at
about the same point in the day, whether or not anybody is watching a shadow. One mark before it and
one after it is all the time-keeping this needs — a then and a now.

Now there are two marks — each where the light stopped — and a gap between them, and the gap is
a certain number of finger-widths across. That number is the world's, not hers. Her part was to
write it down.

That is the whole of what a measurement is: something that changes, and someone willing to record
by how much.

So she has a number. Does she have an answer?

## A number is not yet an answer

<!-- beat the-shadow.3 -->

No. And it is worth being exact about why, because this is the gap the rest of the book lives in.

The two marks are compatible with almost anything. A sun that moves. A ground that tilts. A stick
that grows. She has no way to prefer one of those to the others, because nothing in the marks
themselves says which. A measurement on its own is a number with a time attached — true, and mute.

What turns it into an answer is a *reason to expect one thing rather than another*, written down
before you look. That is the harder half, it is the half people skip, and somebody did it with a
shadow a very long time ago.

Did he have to be a genius?

## What Eratosthenes did next

<!-- beat the-shadow.4 -->

Around 240 BCE a man in Alexandria did the next thing, starting from almost exactly this.

Eratosthenes of Cyrene ran the library at Alexandria, the largest collection of books in the world.
The measurement asked for none of it. It needed an upright post, the length of its shadow at noon, a
distance somebody had already walked, and one multiplication — the kind you could do in your head,
given the two numbers. So, no: anyone with the two numbers could have done it. The library is the
part of him you can leave out, and that is what makes him a good first example.

What he did need was a puzzle: a discrepancy he could not explain.

## Two posts, one shadow

<!-- beat the-shadow.5 -->

The discrepancy was a story about a well. At Syene, far to the south, at noon on the longest day of
the year, sunlight was said to fall straight down a deep well and light the water at the bottom.
Straight down. An upright post standing beside that well threw no shadow at all.

In Alexandria, on the same day and at the same hour, an upright post *did* throw a shadow. A short
one, but a real one.

Two upright posts. Same day, same hour, same Sun. One casts a shadow; the other does not.

On a flat world that cannot happen. Upright posts on a flat world, lit by a Sun far enough away that
its rays arrive parallel, all lean their shadows the same way by the same amount — so if one of them
has no shadow, none of them do.

The ground between the two towns is therefore not flat. It curves. But by how much?

## How much it curves

<!-- beat the-shadow.6 -->

Here he turned the shape into a number, which is what made the shape able to be wrong.

He measured the angle of the Alexandria shadow, and it came out at about a fiftieth of a full turn.
That angle is doing all the work, because it is also the angle between the two towns as seen from
the centre of the Earth. If a fiftieth of the circle separates them, then the whole circle is fifty
times the distance between them.

He still needed that distance, and he looked it up: the figure in use for Alexandria to Syene was
around five thousand stadia.

Fifty times five thousand. One multiplication, and he had a size for the Earth — from two shadows,
a distance somebody had walked, and the geometry of a circle. He was close, and everything he used,
a child in a yard could have used.

He kept an order this book keeps: the shape first, then a number that could have come out absurd
and sunk it, and only then the multiplication. The middle step — saying what would sink you, before
you look — is the one people skip.

So put the child back in the yard, with her stick and her two marks, and ask what she is still
missing.

## Back to the child

<!-- beat the-shadow.7 -->

She has done the noticing, and she has a number for how much. What is still to come is small: a
structure she can hold in her head, and a statement of what it would do that could turn out false.

That is the hard part, and it is what the rest of this book does: builds a small world a little at a
time, says what it should do before it does it, and lets it answer. The one thing to carry in with
you is that a mark can be faithful and still be too coarse to settle what was asked of it — which is
worth knowing before you make one.

So this is where she hands the book over. She got you this far.
